## Purpose

This assignment replaces a traditional peer review with a user testing process drawn directly from Drucker's Exercise 10.8. Rather than having a classmate write a general impression of your site, you will design specific questions for your testers to answer. Your testers then work through your site responding to those questions.

This mirrors how interface design actually gets evaluated: the designer defines what they want to know, and testers respond to those specific questions based on their experience of the site.

## Part A: Testing Strategy (written by the site author)

**Due with your site: Monday, April 13 at 9:00 AM**

Submit a short testing strategy alongside your site. This is a set of specific questions you want your testers to answer. Share the live URL of your site and this document with your testers by April 13.

Suggested filename: `testing-strategy.md`

### What Makes a Good Testing Question

A good testing question:

1. Targets a specific design decision you made
2. Can be answered by someone navigating the site without guidance from you
3. Has an answer you genuinely do not already know
4. Reveals whether a specific element of the interface is working

A weak testing question:

1. Asks whether the tester liked the site
2. Is too broad to answer specifically ("Is the site good?")
3. Can only be answered by someone who already knows the project

### Testing Strategy Structure

Use these sections in your testing strategy.

**Site URL:** [your live GitHub Pages URL]

**Intended visitor:**  
Describe 1 to 2 specific user profiles for your site. Who is this site designed for? Name their background, what they are looking for, and what they do not already know. Be concrete — not "anyone interested in history," but a specific type of reader or researcher. Your testers will navigate the site as this person.

**What this site is trying to do:**  
One to two sentences describing the interface goal — not the historical topic, but what you want a visitor to understand and do.

**Testing questions:**  
Write 4 to 6 specific questions. At least one must address:

1. Whether your home page communicates the project within 30 seconds
2. Whether your planning page makes your design decisions visible
3. Whether your visualization is interpreted rather than just displayed
4. One decision you were uncertain about when building the site

**What you most want to know:**  
One sentence naming the single thing you are most unsure worked.

## Part B: Tester Response (written by the testers)

**Due: Monday, April 20 at 9:00 AM**

Work through the site author's testing questions. Before you begin, read the author's **intended visitor** description and navigate the site as that person — not as yourself. Do not ask the author for help or clarification before completing your responses.

Suggested filename: `testing-response-[author-first-name].md`

When you finish, email your completed tester response to the site author. The tester response does not need to be submitted separately to the instructor or placed inside the published GitHub Pages site folder.

### Tester Response Structure

**Site tested:** [URL]  
**Author:** [name]  
**Tester:** [your name]

**Responses to testing questions:**  
Answer each of the author's questions in order. For each:

1. State whether you could complete the task
2. Describe what you found and how easy or difficult it was
3. Note what helped or what got in the way

**Additional observations:**  
Note anything significant the testing questions did not cover. Useful areas:

1. Navigation — did you get lost or confused?
2. Accessibility — broken images, missing alt text, contrast issues
3. Interpretation — visualization shown without context, unclear argument
4. Sustainability page — if the site includes one, was its purpose understandable?

**One question for the author:**  
Ask one question that would help the author clarify a design or interpretive decision.

### Standards for Tester Responses

A strong response:

1. Answers each testing question specifically and honestly
2. Points to actual pages, elements, or text
3. Notes both what worked and what did not
4. Is useful to the author in deciding what to revise

A weak response:

1. Gives vague impressions instead of specific observations
2. Focuses on aesthetic preferences rather than whether the interface worked
3. Skips questions or gives one-word answers
